# UX laws

Paraphrased from lawsofux.com. 30 entries. Column "Use in wireframe" is how the skill applies it.

## Decision and choice
| Law | Meaning | Use in wireframe |
|---|---|---|
| Hick's Law | Decision time grows with number and complexity of choices. Hick 1952. | One primary CTA per screen. Nav 5 to 7 items. Group options. |
| Choice Overload | Too many options stall decisions. | Max 3 pricing tiers, mark one recommended. |
| Occam's Razor | Pick the option with fewest assumptions. | Remove any block that has no job. |
| Pareto Principle | ~20% of causes give ~80% of effects. | Put the top 20% tasks in the first screen. |

## Motor and time
| Law | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Fitts's Law | Smaller or farther targets take longer to hit. Fitts 1954. | Primary button large, near the content it acts on. Touch targets 44px+. |
| Doherty Threshold | Under ~400 ms response keeps people in flow. | Skeletons, optimistic UI. |
| Parkinson's Law | Tasks expand to fill time given. | Show short forms and progress. |

## Memory
| Law | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Miller's Law | Working memory holds ~7 ± 2 items. Modern view is ~4 chunks (Cowan 2001). | Chunk long content into groups of 3 to 5. |
| Chunking | Group pieces into meaningful units. | Phone numbers, steps, cards. |
| Working Memory | Temporary store for task info. | Never make users remember data from a previous screen. |
| Serial Position Effect | First and last items are remembered best. | Key nav items at ends. Strongest benefit first, CTA last. |
| Von Restorff Effect | The item that differs is remembered. | Only the primary CTA gets the accent color. |
| Zeigarnik Effect | Unfinished tasks stay on the mind. | Progress bars, "2 of 4 done". |
| Peak-End Rule | Experiences judged by the peak and the end. | Design the success state and the confirmation page. |

## Perception (Gestalt)
| Law | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Law of Proximity | Close items read as a group. | Spacing scale: tight inside a group, loose between groups. |
| Law of Common Region | Items in a shared boundary read as related. | Cards, panels. |
| Law of Similarity | Similar looking items read as a group. | Same style for same function. |
| Law of Uniform Connectedness | Connected items read as more related. | Lines, steppers. |
| Law of Prägnanz | People see the simplest form. | Simple shapes, clear grid. |
| Aesthetic-Usability Effect | Attractive designs seem easier to use. | Polish matters even at mid fi. |

## Expectations and behaviour
| Law | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Jakob's Law | Users expect your site to work like others they know. | Logo top left, cart top right, search at top. |
| Mental Model | Users carry a simplified picture of how it works. | Use their words, not internal names. |
| Paradox of the Active User | People skip manuals and start using. | Inline hints, no tutorial walls. |
| Postel's Law | Accept input loosely, output strictly. | Forgiving form fields (spaces, formats). |
| Tesler's Law | Some complexity can only be moved, not removed. | Move it to the system: defaults, autofill. |
| Goal-Gradient Effect | People speed up near the goal. | Show remaining steps, start progress at a non zero point. |
| Flow | Deep focus state. | Remove interruptions mid task. |
| Selective Attention | People notice what matches their goal. | Avoid banner looking blocks for key content (banner blindness). |
| Cognitive Bias | Predictable thinking errors. | Use ethically: anchoring in pricing, defaults. No dark patterns. |
| Cognitive Load | Mental effort to use the interface. | See Cognitive Load and Memory. |

## Evidence strength
Hick, Fitts, Miller, Aesthetic-Usability have lab research behind them. Jakob's Law and Postel's Law are expert heuristics. Treat the second group as strong defaults, not laws of nature.

Sources: lawsofux.com · Hick 1952 "On the rate of gain of information" (Q. J. Exp. Psych.) · Fitts 1954 · Miller 1956 Psych. Review 63:81-97 · Cowan 2001 Behav. Brain Sci. 24:87-114
